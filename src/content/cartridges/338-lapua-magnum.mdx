name: ".338 Lapua Magnum"
---

The .338 Lapua Magnum was drawn up in 1983 by the US Research Armament Company as a conversion of
the .416 Rigby for a US Navy long-range sniping trial, then finished and put into production by
Lapua in Finland in 1989. The brief was a round that still held its accuracy and a
useful share of its muzzle energy at 1,000 metres and beyond, fired from a rifle a two-man team
could carry, which is a gap the belted magnums of the day did not fill and the .50 BMG filled only
at several times the weight.

The case is unbelted and rimless, 69.2 mm long with a shallow 13.82-degree shoulder, holding an
8.61 mm bullet nearly 20 mm longer in overall length than a .308 Winchester round. That length is
the point of the cartridge: it lets a heavy, high-ballistic-coefficient boat-tailed bullet sit
without eating into powder capacity, so a rifle built around it is close in size and weight to a
7.62 mm system while retaining supersonic flight and useful energy well past where a 7.62x51mm
round has gone transonic. NATO issue rifles chambered for it fire it under the reporting name
7.62x67mm.

Two Lapua factory loads are recorded here, a 16.2 g (250 gr) and a 19.4 g (300 gr) Scenar
open-tip-match bullet, both tested from Lapua's own 680 mm barrel — a rifle-length barrel, not the
carbine lengths this site records for most service rifle cartridges, and consistent with the
round's role in purpose-built precision rifles rather than general-issue carbines.

Gaps on this page: no cartridge mass (loaded round, not bullet) is published by Lapua for either
load. No photograph of the round chambered or fired is recorded; the gallery here is two studio
specimen shots and one photograph of fired cases from a 2016 U.S. Marine Corps caliber study,
rather than a picture of the cartridge in a rifle.
