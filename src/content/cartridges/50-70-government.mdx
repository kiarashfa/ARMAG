The .50-70 Government is a rimmed, straight-walled centerfire rifle cartridge the United States
Army adopted in 1866, the first standard metallic-cartridge round it issued after the Civil War.
It replaced the muzzle-loading rifle-muskets that had equipped Union and Confederate infantry,
firing from breech-loading trapdoor conversions of existing Springfield rifle-muskets and, later,
purpose-built trapdoor Springfields. Like other cartridges of its generation named on the American
pattern, its name pairs the bullet's nominal diameter in hundredths of an inch with a second number that
described the original loading rather than any dimension of the cartridge.

The Army replaced it as its standard rifle cartridge in 1873 with the smaller-bored .45-70
Government, and the round's own military service accordingly ran only from 1866 to 1873, though
.50-70 rifles and ammunition remained in wider circulation afterward. It used a Benét internal
primer, a design that concealed the priming compound inside the case head rather than in an
exposed primer pocket.

Case and pressure dimensions here come from C.I.P.'s published standard and are recorded as
verified. Three historical bullet weights are recorded from a handloading data page cited by
Wikipedia's infobox, but that page's published velocities are maximum-handload results tied to a
specific powder charge rather than factory-ammunition figures, so no velocity or energy is
recorded for this cartridge. No period photograph of a trapdoor Springfield rifle chambered for
it appears on this site.
