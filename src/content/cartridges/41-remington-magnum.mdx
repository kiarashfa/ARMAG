The .41 Remington Magnum was Remington's 1964 answer to a problem three well-known handgunners had
been arguing over: the .357 Magnum's law-enforcement loads were, in their view, too light to be a
reliable stopper, while the .44 Magnum's recoil was more than most officers could shoot well under
pressure. Elmer Keith, Bill Jordan and Skeeter Skelton pushed Remington and Smith & Wesson toward a
round in between the two, built on a lengthened .41-calibre rimmed straight case loaded to a
substantially lower pressure than the .44 Magnum.

It never became the police cartridge its designers hoped for — heavy-for-caliber police loads still
produced more recoil than many departments wanted, and by the time factory ammunition settled into a
milder standard loading, most agencies had already moved toward semi-automatic pistols rather than
adopting a new revolver cartridge at all. It has instead persisted as a hunting and handgun-hunting
round, loaded from a fairly mild 170-grain jacketed hollow-point up to substantially heavier
240-and-265-grain loads intended for larger game.

Every dimension in this entry comes from the C.I.P. datasheet for "41 Rem. Mag.", and all four
factory loads carry a velocity measured from a stated 6.5-inch test barrel by Ballistics by the
Inch, an independent chronograph-testing site cited directly in Wikipedia's own infobox rather than
relayed through it — each velocity reproduces its load's published energy figure through ½mv² to
within a tenth of a percent. No ballistic coefficient is published for any of the four loads, so no
trajectory beyond muzzle figures can be computed for this cartridge.
