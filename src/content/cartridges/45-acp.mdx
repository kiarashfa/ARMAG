Browning designed it in 1904 for a US Army that had decided, after the Philippines, that it wanted a
large-calibre service pistol again. The specification was a .45 bullet; what he built around it was a
straight rimless case running at low pressure — 130 MPa, less than a third of a rifle round — and moving a
very heavy bullet slowly.

That combination is the whole character of the cartridge and of the pistol built for it. Low pressure
means a simple tilting-barrel lock and a slide that does not need to be heavy. A 15 g bullet at 250 m/s
means recoil that arrives as a push rather than a snap, which is why the round remains popular in
competition a century after the army that commissioned it moved on.
