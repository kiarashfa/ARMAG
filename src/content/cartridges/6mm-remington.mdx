The 6mm Remington began life in 1955 as the .244 Remington, a rimless bottleneck round Fred Huntington and
Mike Walker built by necking the .257 Roberts case — itself a necked-down 7×57mm Mauser — down to take a
.24 calibre bullet. Remington chambered its Model 722 for the round with a 1-in-12-inch twist chosen to
suit a 90-grain soft-point big-game bullet without over-spinning lighter varmint loads, a dual-purpose
brief aimed at the same niche as the .243 Winchester, introduced the same year.

The two cartridges started from the same idea and diverged in reception rather than case design.
Winchester's .243 offered a faster 1-in-10-inch twist that could stabilise heavier 100-grain bullets and
sold better from the outset; Remington's original slow twist gave the .244 a real edge with light,
high-velocity varmint bullets but limited how heavy a bullet it could shoot accurately, and it further
struggled in the marketplace on a widespread but disputed belief that it was a light varmint round rather
than a deer cartridge in its own right. Remington renamed it the 6mm Remington in 1963 and rechambered
later rifles with a faster twist to stabilise the same heavier bullets its rival handled from the start,
but it has never matched the .243 Winchester's commercial popularity, remaining instead a round favoured
by handloaders and custom rifle builders for the small ballistic edge it retains at long range.

No free dimensional standard for the 6mm Remington was found — it does not appear in the C.I.P.
publication despite being a SAAMI-standardised American round — so every dimension on this page is
recorded as estimated from Wikipedia's uncited infobox rather than verified against a primary source. The
only bullet-mass and load data found came from a handloading reference (Accurate Powder), whose velocity
figures reflect a specific propellant charge rather than a fact about the cartridge itself and are not
recorded here; no factory-ammunition source with a stated test-barrel velocity was found either, so this
entry carries bullet masses without velocities and no muzzle energy.
