The .22 Long arrived in 1871 as a longer-cased answer to the .22 Short: the same rimmed,
straight, 5.72 mm bullet, but a case lengthened by roughly 5 mm to hold more black powder and
push the same bullet faster. It was built for rifles rather than pocket revolvers, and it
extended the .22 rimfire's reach at a point when the Short's mild report and low recoil had
already made the caliber the default choice for target and gallery shooting.

Its own market position did not last. In 1887 the .22 Long Rifle combined the .22 Long's case
with a heavier bullet, and within a few decades the newer round had taken over almost every role
the Long had held. The .22 Long survives today mainly as a low-cost, low-recoil option still
loaded by a handful of manufacturers, largely for shooters using older firearms chambered
specifically for it, rather than as a first choice for anyone buying rimfire ammunition new.

This entry's case, bullet and pressure dimensions are transcribed from the current C.I.P.
datasheet and are `verified`. No source found states a muzzle velocity for this cartridge
alongside the barrel length it was measured from, so no load figure is recorded — a velocity
with no barrel length behind it is not a measurement this site can use. The photograph is a
period comparison shot showing the .22 Long beside the .22 Long Rifle that displaced it; no
image of the .22 Long on its own, isolated, was found under a free licence.
