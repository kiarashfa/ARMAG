name: "6.5mm Creedmoor"
---

The 6.5mm Creedmoor was drawn up at Hornady in 2007 by Dave Emary with Dennis DeMille of Creedmoor
Sports, for competitors who wanted a long, high-drag-efficiency 6.5 mm bullet out of an unmodified
short bolt action. It is a .30 Thompson Center necked down, and almost every decision in it is about
where the bullet sits rather than about how fast it goes.

The constraint is the magazine. A short action feeds a cartridge about 71 mm long, and the C.I.P.
maximum overall length here is 71.76 mm — so the case had to be cut back to 48.77 mm to leave the
bullet somewhere to be. The neck that remains is long enough that even a 140-grain match bullet can
be seated to magazine length with its base still inside the neck rather than down in the body, which
is the whole reason for the geometry: a bullet that sits below the neck-shoulder junction eats powder
capacity and, on a case reloaded many times, sits against the thickened brass that forms there. The
shoulder is steep, 30 degrees a side, and the head is the .308 Winchester head at 11.95 mm, so a
rifle changes chambering with a barrel and nothing else.

That last property is what took it out of competition. In October 2017 US Special Operations Command
tested it against 7.62x51mm NATO and .260 Remington in SR-25, M110A1 and Mk 20 rifles, and reported
roughly double the hit probability at 1,000 m with about a third less wind drift; because the two
rounds share magazines and bolt face, converting a semi-automatic sniper rifle meant a barrel swap.
Adoption followed in 2019, and the round has since been given the US designation XM1200.

Gaps on this page: the two loads recorded here are the encyclopedia's own ballistics table, which
states a single 28-inch test barrel for every row and names Hornady, SAAMI and C.I.P. as its sources
without saying which row came from which — so both velocities are attributed to the article rather
than to a manufacturer datasheet. No ballistic coefficient is recorded, because none of the sources
reachable for free publishes one tied to a specific factory load. The C.I.P. figures transcribed here
are the European maxima; SAAMI's ceiling of 62,000 psi is measured by a different method and is noted
rather than stored as a second value.
