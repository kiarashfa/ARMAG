The .30-30 Winchester was introduced in 1895 as the smokeless-powder chambering for the
Winchester Model 1894 lever-action rifle, and it is usually credited as the first small-bore
sporting cartridge loaded with smokeless powder sold in the United States. Its name is a
leftover of the black-powder era, when a cartridge's name paired its nominal calibre with its
nominal charge rather than a measured bullet diameter — a convention it inherited even though it
was never sold as a black-powder round.

Like other rounds built for a rifle with a tubular magazine, its bullet nose is flat or
gently rounded rather than pointed, so that recoil cannot drive one cartridge's tip into the
primer of the round ahead of it. That constraint has shaped every factory loading of the
cartridge for well over a century, including modern polymer-tipped designs such as Hornady's
FTX and MonoFlex bullets, whose soft, deformable tips are shaped to satisfy the same
magazine-safety requirement while behaving more like a spitzer bullet in flight. The
cartridge's rimmed, gently tapered case and modest 51.8 mm case length reflect a design built
around a lever-action's short receiver and tube magazine rather than long-range ballistics,
and its retained energy at longer ranges falls off accordingly.

Despite its age the .30-30 remains in wide production and is still regarded as an adequate
deer-hunting round at typical woods ranges.

This entry's dimensions and pressure are transcribed from the C.I.P.'s TDCC datasheet. Both
loads carry a manufacturer-stated 24-inch test barrel — Hornady publishes it directly on its
own product pages, unlike the Wikipedia ballistics table for this cartridge, which lists five
loads with no barrel length attached to any of them and so contributes no velocity here. No
SAAMI-published pressure figure was found to compare against the C.I.P. one.
