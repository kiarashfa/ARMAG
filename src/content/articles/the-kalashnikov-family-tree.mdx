Start with a correction. The rifle almost everyone pictures when they hear *AK-47* is not an AK-47. It is an
AKM, or a licensed copy of one, or a copy of a copy. The genuine article — the milled-receiver rifle the Soviet
Army adopted in 1949 — was made for about ten years and replaced in 1959. Every stamped-receiver Kalashnikov
made since is its successor rather than the thing itself.

That confusion is worth untangling, because the pattern of how this design spread is more interesting than the
design.

## Three receivers in twelve years

Mikhail Kalashnikov's rifle won a Soviet trial in 1947 against entries from Bulkin and Dementyev, and its
first production receiver was a stamped sheet-metal shell with a milled insert riveted in. Soviet factories
could not hold the tolerances, rejection rates were unacceptable, and the design was changed to a receiver
milled from a solid billet — stronger, much slower to make, and about half a kilogram heavier.

The stamping only came back in 1959, with the AKM, once the industry could do it. That rifle is lighter, has a
different rear sight, a slanted muzzle brake and a stamped receiver, and it is the one that went everywhere.
The [AK-47](/ARMAG/guns/ak-47/) entry on this site carries the 1949 rifle's figures and says so; its production
total is recorded as an estimate because no manufacturer has ever published a count and the widely repeated
seventy-five million is a survey figure.

## Licensed, copied, and neither

The interesting part is what happened next, and the three different legal and technical relationships involved
are why this site records lineage as typed edges rather than a single "based on" field.

The [Type 56](/ARMAG/guns/type-56/) is **licensed production**. The Soviet Union transferred drawings and
tooling to China in the 1950s, and Norinco built the rifle to them. It is the same rifle made by someone else
with permission, and the differences — a folding spike bayonet, a fully enclosed front sight hood — are
deliberate local changes rather than errors.

The [IWI Galil](/ARMAG/guns/iwi-galil/) is **derived from** it. Israel came out of 1967 having decided the
[FN FAL](/ARMAG/guns/fn-fal/) was too long and too sensitive to sand, ran a trial, and adopted a rifle whose
action is a Kalashnikov's — long-stroke piston, rotating bolt, same basic geometry — licensed from Valmet's
Finnish version rather than copied from a Soviet one. Everything above the receiver is different: the stock
folds sideways, the charging handle is bent up so either hand can work it, there is a second safety lever above
the grip, and the whole thing is chambered for a NATO cartridge.

And then there is everything that is neither: Romanian, Bulgarian, Egyptian, Iraqi, Yugoslav and Pakistani
production of varying legality and varying quality, plus an enormous commercial trade in receivers and parts
kits. This site records what it can attribute and does not guess at the rest.

## What made it copyable

The usual explanation is that the design is simple. That is half right and misses the more useful half.

The Kalashnikov's tolerances are generous — clearances large enough that dirt, ice and badly made ammunition
rarely stop the cycle. The same clearances are why it has never been an accurate rifle by target standards.
Both halves of that trade were deliberate, and the important consequence is industrial rather than tactical: a
rifle designed to work with loose tolerances can be made by a factory that cannot hold tight ones.

That is what a country with a new arms industry needs, and it is why the design propagated through exactly
those countries. Compare the [M16](/ARMAG/guns/m16-rifle/), which is a more accurate rifle made to closer
tolerances, and whose licensed production went to established manufacturers in Singapore, Canada and Belgium
rather than to new ones.

## Reading the lineage graph

Every entry on this site that descends from another carries a typed edge saying how, and those edges have to
resolve to a real entry before the build will pass. That constraint has a visible consequence: the
[Type 56](/ARMAG/guns/type-56/) could only record its `licensedProductionOf` edge once the
[AK-47](/ARMAG/guns/ak-47/) existed here as a page, and the AK-47 was written in the pilot batch specifically so
that the edge would have somewhere to point.

The tree is therefore incomplete, and visibly so. The AKM, the AK-74, the RPK and the dozen national variants
are not here yet, and until they are, the graph on the AK-47's page shows two edges rather than fifty. That is
an accurate picture of what this catalogue currently knows, which is the only kind of picture it is willing to
draw.
