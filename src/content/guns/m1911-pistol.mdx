Browning's short-recoil pistol solved the problem every locked-breech design has to solve — how to
unlock the barrel from the slide after a few millimetres of travel — with a swinging link that pulls the
barrel's rear downwards out of engagement. Everything else about the pistol follows from wanting that
mechanism to be serviceable in a field workshop: it comes apart without tools, and the parts are large.

It won the 1911 US trials by firing six thousand rounds without a malfunction, and the only significant
revision in a century of service was the A1 of 1924, which changed the trigger, the grip safety spur and the
mainspring housing after complaints from the previous war.

Its second life is competitive. A single-action trigger with a short, light break, a frame that indexes the
same way every time, and an aftermarket that has had a hundred years to develop have kept it dominant in
practical and bullseye shooting long after it stopped being a service weapon.
