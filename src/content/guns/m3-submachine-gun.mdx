The M3 is what happens when an ordnance board stops asking for a good submachine gun and starts asking
for a cheap one. Adopted in December 1942 to replace the Thompson, it was drawn by George Hyde around
sheet steel and a car-parts factory rather than around a machine shop, and its receiver is two stamped
halves welded into a tube. Soldiers called it the grease gun, after the tool it resembles.

Almost everything about it follows from the cost target. There is no semi-automatic position, because a
selector is parts; the bolt is instead made deliberately heavy so that the cyclic rate falls to 450 rounds
a minute, slow enough that a trained hand can release the trigger after one round. It fires from an open
bolt using advanced primer ignition, so the cartridge is struck while the bolt is still travelling forward
and part of the bolt's momentum has to be reversed before the case can move — which is what allows a
weapon this light to be simple blowback in a cartridge this large. The bolt is drilled through
longitudinally for two guide rods carrying the return springs, a layout that tolerates loose machining
because the rods, not the receiver walls, hold the bolt in line.

The original M3 is cocked by a crank on the right of the ejector housing. That mechanism was the first
thing to break in service, and the M3A1 of late 1944 deleted it entirely: the bolt has a finger hole cut
into its top face and is pulled back by hand through the open ejector port. The same weapon accepted a
factory kit of barrel, bolt and magazine adapter that rechambered it for 9x19mm Parabellum, and about a
thousand were built that way for the Office of Strategic Services, so that agents could be supplied from
captured German stocks. The double-column, single-feed magazine was copied from the Sten and inherited
the Sten's sensitivity to dirt along with its shape.

It was issued as a disposable item — no unit-level spares, no armourer's tools — and it outlasted that
intention by half a century, riding in American tanks until the 1990s.
