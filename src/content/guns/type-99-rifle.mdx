The Type 99 is the second generation of the Arisaka bolt-action rifle, adopted by the Imperial Japanese
Army in 1939. In the Second Sino-Japanese War the army found the 7.7mm round of its Type 92 heavy
machine gun superior to the 6.5×50mm cartridge of the Type 38 rifle, and it developed the Type 99 from
the Type 38 to fire the larger round and move the infantry onto a single rifle cartridge. Several Type
38s were converted to 7.7mm for trials, and although they worked, the added recoil and the larger
chamber led the army to build an entirely new rifle for it rather than rebarrel the old one.

The action cocks on closing, and its safety is an unusual knurled disc at the rear of the bolt that the
shooter presses in with the palm and turns an eighth of a turn. Early rifles carried a wire monopod,
flip-up anti-aircraft sight arms, a bolt dust cover and a chrome-lined bore, the first on a mass-produced
infantry rifle; all were dropped by the middle of the war. Late-war "last ditch" rifles were simplified
further, with crude stocks, fixed sights and visible tooling marks, though the bolt itself was not
cheapened. The army meant the Type 99 to replace the Type 38 entirely, but the Pacific War never allowed
it, and both served to 1945.

It was built in four versions, the standard short rifle recorded here, the long rifle, the take-down
Type 2 paratroop rifle and a sniper rifle, at nine arsenals in Japan, Korea and Manchuria. After the war
others converted large numbers: about 133,000 were rechambered for .30-06 Springfield under American
supervision for South Korea, the Republic of China converted rifles to 8×57mm and 7.62×39mm, and
Thailand converted some to .30-06. None of those is a chambering the rifle was built for, so none is
recorded as one.

No single arsenal made most Type 99s, so the entry names no maker. Every dimension on this page is an
uncited encyclopedia figure and recorded as an estimate; no source consulted gives a twist rate or states
what the mass figure includes, and the long rifle's barrel length and mass are not published.
