This rifle began as an answer to a problem the Second Boer War exposed: British troops facing accurate
long-range fire from Boer riflemen armed with 7×57mm Mausers. The War Office responded by developing its
own high-velocity round, the .276 Enfield, and a new Mauser-derived bolt action to fire it, the Pattern
1913. War broke out before that rifle or its new cartridge reached mass production, and introducing an
unfamiliar cartridge into a wartime supply chain was judged too disruptive, so the same action was
adapted instead to fire the existing .303 British round already standard across the army — the change
that turned the Pattern 1913 into the Pattern 1914.

Even then it never became Britain's primary rifle. Vickers, the lead contractor, could not scale up
production fast enough, so the Short Magazine Lee-Enfield remained standard issue and American firms —
Winchester, Remington and Eddystone — were contracted to build P14s to supplement supply instead. What the
design offered over the SMLE was its aperture sight, mounted with protective ears and a longer sight
radius than the SMLE's open sights, riding on a bolt action stronger than the SMLE's own; that combination
made the P14 a natural choice as Britain's sniper rifle even while it never displaced the Lee-Enfield as
the standard infantry arm.

Its most lasting legacy came through a cartridge change rather than a doctrine one. When the United
States entered the war, American arsenals reworked the P14 to feed .30-06 Springfield instead of .303
British and built it as the Model of 1917 — a rifle that, in total production, went on to outnumber the
Springfield it was meant to supplement.
