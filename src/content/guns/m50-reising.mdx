Harrington & Richardson brought the Reising into production in 1941 because the US military could not get
enough Thompson submachine guns to go around: the Reising cost a fraction of the Thompson's price and was
lighter and more accurate, so the Marine Corps adopted it as a stopgap while Thompson production caught up
with wartime demand. Its delayed-blowback action works by a tilting bolt rather than a simple mass-and-spring
blowback: the rear of the bolt seats into a curved recess machined into the receiver, and firing pressure
has to push that curved surface back to horizontal before the bolt can travel rearward, buying the fraction
of a second a closed-bolt, delayed design needs to keep chamber pressure safe.

That mechanism, and the close manufacturing tolerances it needed, turned out to be the Reising's undoing in
the field. In the sand, mud and humidity of the Solomon Islands campaign the action fouled easily, and
minor damage to a magazine's feed lips — or grit worked into one — could put it out of action entirely, in
a design where interchangeable parts and field maintenance had not been given the same priority as cost.
Marines came to distrust the gun badly enough that some discarded it for the bolt-action M1903 Springfield
rather than carry a Reising into combat. The folding-stock M55 variant made for paratroopers made an
already troubled design worse: its stock was flimsy enough to fold under recoil while firing.

Two further derivatives are not modelled here. The M60 was a semi-automatic-only carbine built for
training and police sales rather than combat use, which this site's taxonomy classes as a carbine rather
than a submachine gun; and the M65 was a .22 Long Rifle training version built from 1944 to 1946, a
different cartridge family from the M50's .45 ACP. Both are separate designs built on the same action
rather than variants of this entry.
