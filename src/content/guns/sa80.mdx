The SA80 was developed at the Royal Small Arms Factory, Enfield, through the 1970s and 1980s
as Britain's replacement for the L1A1 self-loading rifle, and it adopted the bullpup layout —
the action and magazine set behind the trigger, inside the buttstock — to keep a full-length
barrel within a compact overall length, the same trade-off that drove other European bullpup
rifles of the period such as the FAMAS and Steyr AUG. It entered service in 1985 as the L85
individual weapon, alongside the L86 Light Support Weapon, a heavier-barrelled version of the
same action intended to give a section sustained automatic fire from the same magazine-fed
system, and the shorter L22 carbine built for vehicle crews and other roles where the
full-length rifle was unwieldy.

The rifle's early service life was marked by persistent reliability problems, serious enough
that the design underwent a comprehensive rebuild by Heckler & Koch in the early 2000s,
producing the improved A2 standard that gives this entry's figures. That rebuild replaced or
reworked the magazine, extractor, cocking handle, gas system and other components, and it is
generally credited with resolving the faults that had dogged the original A1-standard rifle in
earlier service, including during the 1991 Gulf War.

Mechanically the SA80 is gas-operated with a rotating bolt, feeding from STANAG-pattern
magazines shared with other 5.56×45mm NATO rifles, and its bullpup layout means the ejection
port sits close to a right-handed firer's face — a well-known limitation of the pattern that
makes it awkward to fire from the left shoulder without a dedicated left-handed ejection
configuration. The rifle mounts optical sights as standard rather than relying on iron sights
alone, originally the SUSAT and more recently the ELCAN LDS or ACOG, reflecting a design
philosophy that assumed an optic from the outset rather than treating it as an add-on.

This entry describes the L85A2 rifle rather than the L86 LSW or L22 carbine, both of which
share its gas-operated rotating-bolt action but differ enough in barrel length, weight and role
that this single entry does not attempt to give them their own figures. Most figures here come
from Wikipedia's infobox: the weight figure's own cited source, a UK Ministry of Defence PDF,
no longer resolves and no working archived copy was found, and the effective-range figure's
cited source, the British Army's own equipment page, returned an access error rather than the
page itself, so both are recorded as estimated rather than verified. No muzzle velocity is
recorded because no source found ties a published velocity figure to a stated barrel length.
