The G36 is the rifle with which Heckler & Koch gave up on the roller-delayed blowback that had defined
the company since the G3. Submitted for trials as the HK50 and adopted by the Bundeswehr in 1997, it is
a short-stroke gas piston rifle with a rotating bolt — the conventional layout the firm had spent forty
years avoiding — wrapped in a carbon-fibre-reinforced polyamide shell that made it more than a kilogram
lighter than the battle rifle it replaced.

The polymer is the design, not the finish. Receiver housing, stock, trigger group, magazine well,
handguard and carry handle are all moulded, with a steel trunnion set into the receiver to carry the
locking recesses; the barrel is fastened to that trunnion with a nut and floats free of the handguard.
The sight bridge is moulded as one piece with an integral 3x telescope and a reflex sight above it,
which is why the standard rifle has no rail and why the export G36V, sold with a single 1.5x sight,
needed a different bridge rather than a different mount. The magazine is proprietary and translucent,
with studs on its sides so that two or three clip together edge to edge in a soldier's pouch.

The plastic construction is also the source of the rifle's public difficulty. From 2012 the Bundeswehr
reported that sustained firing heated the barrel and its surrounding structure enough to shift the point
of impact, and after tests by the Fraunhofer Ernst-Mach-Institut and a ministerial dispute over whether
the cause was the weapon or the ammunition, Germany opened the Sturmgewehr replacement competition that
eventually selected the HK416 as the G95A1. The G36 remained in service throughout, and remains a
widespread export: Spain adopted it in 1998 and built 75,219 rifles under licence at La Coruña between
1999 and 2005.
