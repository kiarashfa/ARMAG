The Welrod is a British bolt-action pistol designed in 1942 by Major Hugh Reeves at the Inter-Services
Research Bureau's Station IX, in Welwyn — the source of its name, a deliberately misleading combination of
"Wel" (the prefix given to all of Station IX's clandestine equipment) and "rod", a slang term for a gun.
It was built for the Special Operations Executive and resistance groups working in occupied Europe, and its
whole design serves one purpose: to be as quiet and as unremarkable as possible.

The pistol takes the form of a plain metal tube. Its rear section holds the bolt, its middle section a
ported barrel that bleeds propellant gas gradually into an expansion chamber rather than releasing it all
at the muzzle, and its front section a set of metal baffles and rubber wipes that slow the escaping gas
further still — the working principle later reused in weapons such as the MP5SD and AS Val. Because .32
ACP already travels below the speed of sound in most loadings, the primary Mk II/IIA production version
needed only to quiet the mechanical report; a later 9mm Mk I variant, built after reports of poor field
performance from the .32 ACP version, had to slow a normally supersonic 9×19mm Parabellum loading as well,
which is why it is recorded as a separate chambering on this page rather than a simple rebarrel. The
detachable magazine doubles as the pistol's grip and can be removed entirely, leaving an object with no
obvious pistol grip at all — some resistance groups reportedly called it a "bicycle pump" for this reason.
Birmingham Small Arms, the one maker that has confirmed building it, delivered its Welrods without any
markings identifying the maker or country of origin, adding to the weapon's deniability.

Roughly 2,800 were built during the war and perhaps 14,000 in total including post-war production, and
the type has been reported in use well beyond 1945, including with British special forces during the
Falklands War and the 1991 Gulf War, and with U.S. special operations personnel in Vietnam and Cold War
Berlin. A wartime plan existed to distribute Welrods to resistance fighters across occupied Europe for a
coordinated month of attacks against German security personnel; the plan is thought to have been shelved
in the aftermath of the 1942 assassination of Reinhard Heydrich and the mass reprisals that followed it.
