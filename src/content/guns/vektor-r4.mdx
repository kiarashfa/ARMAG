The R4 replaced South Africa's R1, a locally built FN FAL variant, when it entered service in 1980. Rather
than design a new rifle from nothing, Lyttelton Engineering Works built it as a licensed variant of
Yisrael Galili's IMI Galil, itself already a Kalashnikov-derived design, and reworked it for South African
conditions and manufacture. The stock and magazine were remade in high-strength polymer rather than the
Galil's original materials, the stock was lengthened for the average South African soldier's build, and
the folding carrying handle over the receiver was dropped along with a number of changes to the internal
operating mechanism.

Local licensed production mattered for reasons beyond simple economy: South Africa built the R4 and its
ammunition domestically through most of its production run under circumstances that made buying rifles
abroad far harder than for most armies of its size, though the cited article does not spell
out that context directly.

Denel Land Systems, the successor to Lyttelton Engineering Works, still lists the R4 family among its
products. Two carbine derivatives recorded here as variants, the R5 and the shorter R6, share the R4's
gas-operated action and 5.56mm chambering on progressively shorter barrels; a semi-automatic-only civilian
line sold as the LM4, LM5 and LM6 is not modelled here.
