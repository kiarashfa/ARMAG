The Vickers machine gun was introduced by Vickers Limited in 1912 as a lighter, refined
development of Hiram Maxim's original recoil-operated design, and it became the British
Army's standard heavy machine gun through both World Wars, remaining in front-line service
until 1968 — an unusually long service life for a weapon of its type. It differed from
Maxim's own gun chiefly in construction: Vickers inverted the toggle-lock mechanism and used
higher-strength materials to cut the gun's weight substantially while keeping the same basic
recoil-operated action.

Water-cooled and mounted on a tripod, it was built for sustained fire rather than mobility,
and crews became known for keeping a Vickers firing continuously for hours at a time,
feeding it from 250-round canvas belts and topping up its water jacket as needed — a role
suited to defensive and barrage fire rather than the fast-moving infantry support later
machine guns were built around. Its recoil-operated action was assisted by a muzzle booster
that used residual propellant gas to help drive the barrel rearward, an addition that gave the
gun a notably reliable cycling action even as its ammunition and water supply ran hot.

The Vickers fired the standard British .303 service cartridge and equipped British and
Commonwealth forces, alongside foreign-service variants chambered for other rounds; this entry
describes the .303 British version most associated with British Army service rather than
those foreign chamberings. Vickers Limited itself ceased to exist as an independent company in
1927, merging into Vickers-Armstrongs, but production of the gun that carried its name
continued under related firms for decades afterward.
