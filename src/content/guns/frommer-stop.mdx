The Frommer Stop is a semi-automatic pocket pistol Rudolf Frommer designed in 1911 and Fegyver-
és Gépgyár (FÉG) manufactured in Austria-Hungary from 1912. Unlike most blowback pocket pistols of
its size, it uses a long-recoil, straight-pull, rotating-bolt locking action, with the barrel and
bolt riding on independent return springs — a mechanism more commonly associated with far larger,
harder-recoiling weapons than a compact pistol of this class.

It was designed around a proprietary 7.65mm Frommer cartridge, a hotter-loaded, heavier-bulleted
round not carried on this site, and later a similar proprietary 9mm Frommer loading for an export
variant produced from 1912 to 1929. Both proprietary rounds were dimensionally compatible with
existing commercial ammunition — the standard .32 ACP for the 7.65mm pistol and .380 ACP for the
9mm export version — and the pistol fires either commercial cartridge as well, with correspondingly
less energetic recoil, ejection and rechambering than its intended proprietary loads. It saw
military and police service through both World Wars and the regional conflicts that followed
Austria-Hungary's collapse.
