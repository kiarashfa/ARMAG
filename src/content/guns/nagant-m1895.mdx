Every ordinary revolver has a small gap between the front of the cylinder and the back of the barrel,
needed so the cylinder can rotate freely, and that gap vents a jet of hot propellant gas sideways with
every shot — close enough to a shooter's supporting hand to be a known hazard of the type. Léon Nagant's
M1895 closes that gap mechanically: cocking the hammer, either by hand or through the double-action
trigger pull, pushes the entire cylinder forward so its front face seats against a short conical
extension at the rear of the barrel, sealing the joint before the round fires.

That seal only works with a cartridge built for it. Unlike an ordinary revolver round, the Nagant's
7.62×38mmR case has its bullet seated entirely inside the case with the mouth of the brass rolled in to
grip it, rather than the bullet's tip protruding as it does from almost every other cartridge. The
cartridge mouth is what the barrel's conical extension presses against to complete the seal, and the
arrangement gains the round some additional velocity from the sealed cylinder gap on top of solving the
gas-jet problem. The mechanical cost is a startlingly heavy trigger: about 12 lbs of pull to cock the
hammer by hand for single-action fire, and roughly 20 lbs through the full double-action stroke, since
that same pull has to push the cylinder forward against the seal as well as rotate it and cock the hammer.

Belgian-designed but built in far greater numbers under Imperial Russian and later Soviet licence at the
Tula and Izhevsk arsenals, the M1895 stayed in Soviet service alongside the Tokarev pistol for decades and
saw use from the Russo-Japanese War through the Vietnam War in the hands of various forces that inherited
Soviet-bloc stocks.
