The pistol that Gaston Glock's firm submitted to the Austrian army's 1980 trial had a polymer frame, a
striker in place of a hammer, a trigger-mounted safety lever in place of a manual one, and thirty-four
parts. Every one of those choices had been tried before by somebody; the Glock 17 is remembered because
all four arrived together in a design that won a state contract and then survived three decades of
police procurement without a mechanical reputation problem.

Its name is a patent number, not a magazine capacity. That the seventeenth Glock patent produced a
seventeen-round magazine is a coincidence the company has never bothered to correct.

Note what this page cannot tell you. Glock publishes a technical data sheet for the current fifth
generation and nothing comparable for the four that came before, so every measurement on this page
belongs to the Gen5 and the generation tabs record what changed without figures to put against it. The maker also
publishes no rifling twist rate, which is why the trajectory panel on this page cannot say how fast the
bullet is spinning when it leaves.
