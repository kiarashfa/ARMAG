Remington engineer Mike Walker designed the Model 700 in 1962 to replace the Models 721 and 722, which had
themselves been built to undercut the cost of Remington's earlier Model 30. Walker's cost savings came
from how the action was made rather than from cutting anything a shooter would notice: the receiver is
turned from cylindrical bar stock on a lathe instead of being machined in a series of milling operations,
and small parts like the bottom metal are stamped rather than individually cut. None of that came at the
expense of accuracy — Walker held tight tolerances in the chamber and bore, used a short leade ahead of the
chamber, and sped up the lock time between trigger release and firing pin strike, the combination that
made the rifle popular with hunters and target shooters despite its economical manufacture.

That accuracy is also why the design left the sporting market. The Model 700P, a heavier-barrelled police
and tactical variant, became the starting point for two American military sniper rifles built on the same
action: the Marine Corps' M40, in service since 1966, and the Army's M24 Sniper Weapon System, adopted in
1986. Both keep the Model 700's two-lug rotating bolt and manual repeating action rather than replacing it
with anything more complex, on the reasoning that a bolt action's mechanical simplicity is itself an
accuracy advantage over any self-loading design.

Remington has offered the Model 700 in more than two dozen factory chamberings across its production run,
from .222 Remington up to .458 Winchester Magnum. This entry records .308 Winchester as its primary
chambering, reflecting the cartridge shared with the M24 and M40 military derivatives, and .30-06
Springfield as a widely offered civilian alternative; the article does not state which cartridge the
original 1962 rifle launched in.
