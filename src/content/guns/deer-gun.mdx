The Deer gun was a single-shot 9×19mm Parabellum pistol developed by the Central Intelligence Agency as a successor to the FP-45 Liberator. It was meant for distribution to South Vietnamese irregulars for use against North Vietnamese forces, and it never saw actual service. A Forgotten Weapons write-up says the first intention was to take Liberators out of storage, but they had all been scrapped in 1947 and 1948, so a new gun was designed to the same brief of simplicity and low cost. Where the Liberator was stamped, the Deer gun was made mainly from a single aluminium casting.

The receiver is a cylinder at the top of the frame, with a striker that protrudes from the rear and is pulled back to cock. There is no mechanical safety: a small plastic clip fitted around the striker stops it moving forward, and is removed before firing and placed on the barrel, where it serves as the sight. A groove along a ramp on top is the only other aiming aid. Loading is done by unscrewing the barrel, placing a cartridge in the chamber, cocking and screwing the barrel back on, and the same unscrewing, with the supplied rod, clears the spent case. The hollow, checkered grip stores three spare rounds and the rod. The trigger is aluminium and has no guard. Nothing on the gun identified its maker or its user, in order to prevent tracing, and each came in an unmarked polystyrene box with three 9mm rounds and a pictorial instruction sheet.

A production run of 1,000 was made in 1964 as an initial batch, with the final cost projected at US$3.95 a gun. Wikipedia's infobox gives American Machine & Foundry as manufacturer, and a Forgotten Weapons write-up says a contract was given to AMF to build 1,000, dating the run to 1962 rather than 1964; the two sources disagree on the year, and the 1964 figure is used here. The Vietnam War became a full-scale war rather than a small clandestine one, so the gun was less useful than foreseen. Some were evaluated in Vietnam, the fate of the rest is unknown, and most sources state that most were destroyed.
