Eugene Stoner designed the AR-10 at ArmaLite in the mid-1950s to compete for the US Army's rifle
replacement programme, which the M14 would eventually win. The rifle reached the trials stage but an
experimental aluminium barrel with a steel liner burst during a Springfield Armory torture test in early
1957; ArmaLite substituted a conventional steel barrel within weeks, but the Army's evaluators judged the
design would need years more testing before it could be adopted and passed it over. What made the AR-10
distinctive was how light it was for a 7.62mm NATO battle rifle: an aluminium-alloy receiver and a
fibreglass-reinforced pistol grip, handguard and buttstock, materials ArmaLite carried over from its
aircraft-industry parent company, kept it well under most contemporaries' weight in the same chambering.

The design outlived its American rejection through licensed production. Artillerie-Inrichtingen in the
Netherlands began building it under licence from July 1957, producing several variants for export —
Sudan bought around 2,500 rifles in 1958, and Portugal acquired several thousand more from 1960 for its
paratroopers, who carried them through its colonial conflicts in Africa. The rifle Stoner built next, a
scaled-down 5.56mm derivative that Colt would sell to the US Army as the M16, took the AR-10's aluminium
receiver, its in-line stock and its bolt-and-carrier gas system and carried them forward far more widely
than the AR-10 itself ever achieved.
