Ole Herman Krag ran the Norwegian state arms factory at Kongsberg and did not like tube magazines. With
the gunsmith Erik Jørgensen he built an alternative: a horizontal box lying alongside the receiver, fed
through a hinged door on the right, with a follower arm that sweeps the rounds up and across into the
bolt's path. Three countries adopted it — Denmark in 1889, the United States in 1892, Norway in 1894 —
and the figures on this page are the Norwegian M/1894 long rifle, because the Danish and American Krags
fire cartridges this database does not yet hold.

The magazine is the whole argument. Because the door opens sideways rather than up through the action,
the rifle can be topped up at any time without working the bolt and without disturbing a chambered
round, and loose rounds can be thrown in by the handful rather than pressed down against a follower
spring. The price is that they can only go in one at a time: no charger or clip was ever developed for
it, and by 1900 every rival service rifle loaded five rounds in one motion. That trade was defensible in
1889 and indefensible by 1905, which is why the United States replaced the Krag with the Springfield
M1903 after eleven years while Norway, whose doctrine assumed a rifleman had time between reloads, kept
it until 1940.

The bolt is the other half of the design and the reason the Krag feels unlike a Mauser. It has a single
forward locking lug with the bolt-guide rib acting as a safety shoulder, so the locking surfaces sit
asymmetrically between roughly three and six o'clock; the result is a famously smooth, light bolt throw
and an action that will not take a modern high-pressure cartridge. Kongsberg later chambered civilian
Krags for a rimless 8 mm round made by opening the neck of the service case, precisely so that the
pressure stayed where the action could hold it.

Norwegian production ran from 1895 to 1940 in a long rifle, four patterns of carbine, a boy's carbine
for school shooting and three sharpshooter models, with a further batch assembled under German
occupation; the last rifle left Kongsberg in 1956. In Norway it long outlasted its service life as the
standard weapon of civilian marksmanship clubs.

Gaps on this page: no free source publishes dimensions for the Norwegian Krags model by model except the
Norwegian-language encyclopedia, whose per-model list carries no inline citations, so every length and
weight here is recorded as an estimate rather than as a published figure. The English-language article
gives only bands — 986 to 1328 mm overall, 520 to 832 mm of barrel — that span three national families
and are useless as a specification. Rifling twist and groove count for the Norwegian rifle are not
recorded at all; the 1911 Encyclopaedia Britannica's comparative table publishes both, but for the Danish
M/89 in 8 mm, which is a different barrel. Production is given for the M/1894 alone, not for the design.
