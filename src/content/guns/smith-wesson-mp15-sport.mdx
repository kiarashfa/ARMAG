Smith & Wesson's Sport line is the volume end of the AR-15 market: a rifle built to a price, sold on the
strength of the standard it conforms to rather than on anything proprietary. The third generation
arrived in 2024 with a free-floating handguard, a mid-length gas system and a one-in-eight barrel twist,
which between them are the specification a buyer would previously have paid a gunsmith to assemble.

It is in the pilot batch to find out what a long-tail entry actually looks like, and the answer is
instructive. The maker publishes a one-page specification sheet with eleven numbers on it and no
photograph anyone else may reuse; there is no service manual, no independent measurement and no
production figure. Even the price is a problem: the field on this site is for a launch price and what
Smith & Wesson publishes is today's price, so the year beside the figure is this year rather than 2024.

The image below is not this rifle. It shows the previous Sport II with parts its owner fitted, and it is
here because it is the closest freely licensed photograph of the pattern that exists. Saying so is
cheaper than pretending otherwise, and it is what the caption is for.
