Miroslav Rybář designed the vz. 61 for personnel Czechoslovakia judged did not need a full-length rifle
or submachine gun but wanted more than a pistol: lower-ranking staff, vehicle and armoured-vehicle crews,
and special forces working in plain clothes. Its small size follows directly from that role rather than
from any attempt to match a standard submachine gun's power — it chambers .32 ACP, the Czechoslovak
security forces' standard sidearm cartridge, a round mild enough in recoil that the design needs no more
than a simple unlocked blowback action to handle it safely.

That same mildness created a different problem: a gun this light, cycling an unlocked blowback bolt,
would ordinarily fire at a rate too fast to control in short bursts. The Škorpion's answer sits inside its
grip, a rate-reducing mechanism rather than anything in the barrel or bolt. Each time the bolt travels
rearward it catches a spring-loaded hook that drives a plunger down through a free-moving weight in the
grip; the weight's own inertia leaves it behind for a moment, and that lag holds the bolt back briefly
before it can return, dropping the cyclic rate from what would otherwise be close to 1,000 rounds per
minute down to a more manageable rate.

Production ran in two separate periods — an original Czechoslovak run from 1961 to 1979, and a revival at
Česká zbrojovka Uherský Brod from 2009 — and the design was also built under licence by Yugoslavia's
Zastava Arms. It has turned up in conflicts far removed from its original vehicle-crew role, favoured for
its concealability by irregular and paramilitary users from the 1970s onward.
