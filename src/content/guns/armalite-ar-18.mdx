ArmaLite designed the AR-18 to make money a second time from a rifle concept it had already lost once: once
Colt held the AR-15/M16 licence, ArmaLite could not profit further from that design, so Arthur Miller
scaled down the earlier AR-16 into a new 5.56mm rifle in 1963 that did not infringe Colt's rights.
Where the AR-15 depended on forged and precisely machined aluminium, the AR-18 was built largely from
stamped and pinned sheet steel, a construction any licensee with modest tooling could reproduce without
Colt's supply chain or its close machining tolerances — the point of the design was that a country could
build it locally rather than depend on imports.

No major military adopted it. American trials in 1964 found its reliability varied between ammunition
lots, and British trials in 1966 judged it unsatisfactory in mud and sand; by the time either concluded,
the M16 already filled the role a rifle like it would have taken. What the AR-18 achieved instead was a
long licensing history: Howa built it in Japan from 1970 to 1974 and later developed it into the Type
89, Sterling Armaments built it in Britain from 1979 to 1985, and it was reworked into other rifles
including Singapore's SAR-80 and prototypes attempted by the Philippines' Elisco Tool. Its stamped-steel
receiver and rotating bolt on twin operating rods, driven by a short-stroke gas piston rather than the
AR-15's direct-impingement system, became a recognisable mechanical lineage of its own, echoed later in
rifles like the FN FNC and the SA80.
