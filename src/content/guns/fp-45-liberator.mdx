The FP-45 Liberator is a single-shot, break-open .45 ACP pistol George Hyde designed in May 1942
after a Polish military attaché suggested the concept, built to be dropped in great quantities
into occupied territory for use by resistance fighters and partisans. The plan behind it was as
much psychological as practical: dropping enough pistols that occupying forces could never
recover them all was meant to unsettle those forces regardless of how many were actually used.
Its design was deliberately minimal, with just 23 largely stamped and turned steel parts chosen
to be cheap and quick to manufacture rather than accurate or durable.

General Motors' Guide Lamp Division, ordinarily a maker of automotive lighting, manufactured about
one million of them between June and August 1942, at a unit cost of roughly two dollars apiece. Its
single chamber holds one .45 ACP round at a time, reloaded by hand after each shot, and each pistol
shipped with ammunition, instructions and a wooden dowel for punching out the spent case. In
practice, US commanders including General Eisenhower's staff saw little practical value in the
mass-drop plan, and fewer than 25,000 of the million produced were ever distributed; most of the
remainder went undistributed and were later dumped at sea or melted down for scrap.
